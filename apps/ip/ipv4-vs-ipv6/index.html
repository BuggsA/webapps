How IPv6 addresses are shortened

Two rules, from RFC 5952. Leading zeros in each group are dropped: 2001:0db8:0000:0000:0000:0000:0000:0001 becomes 2001:db8:0:0:0:0:0:1. Then the longest run of all-zero groups is replaced by ::, once: 2001:db8::1. Letters are written in lower case. The converter above shows any address both ways.

How IPv4 ran out

1981IPv4 defined in RFC 791.
1993CIDR replaces class A, B and C networks, slowing the spending of IPv4.
1995IPv6 first specified, in RFC 1883.
1998IPv6 revised as RFC 2460.
3 February 2011IANA hands its last five /8 blocks to the five regional registries. Its free pool of IPv4 is empty.
15 April 2011APNIC, the Asia-Pacific registry, reaches its final /8.
8 June 2011World IPv6 Day: major sites turn IPv6 on for 24 hours.
6 June 2012World IPv6 Launch: they leave it on.
14 September 2012RIPE NCC, for Europe and the Middle East, reaches its final /8.
10 June 2014LACNIC, for Latin America, reaches its final /10.
24 September 2015ARIN, for North America, runs out of free IPv4.
2017AFRINIC, for Africa, enters its exhaustion phase.
2017IPv6 becomes a full Internet Standard, RFC 8200.
25 November 2019RIPE NCC makes its last ordinary /22 allocation.

Questions

What is the difference between IPv4 and IPv6?

IPv4 addresses are 32 bits long, so there are about 4.3 billion of them, and they have all been handed out. IPv6 addresses are 128 bits, enough for every device to have its own. IPv6 also simplifies the packet header and drops broadcast. The two are not compatible on the wire: a device needs one or the other, or both, to reach a site.

Is IPv6 better than IPv4?

For the internet as a whole, yes: it removes the shortage that forced homes and phone networks behind shared addresses. For one connection, neither is reliably faster or safer. Speed depends on the route, and both carry the same encrypted traffic.

Which one am I using?

The answer at the top of this page is the one this visit actually used. When your network has both, most devices try IPv6 first and fall back to IPv4 if it answers more slowly.

Can an IPv4 address be written as IPv6?

Yes, in several standard ways. ::ffff:192.0.2.1 is the IPv4-mapped form (RFC 4291), and 64:ff9b::192.0.2.1 is how an IPv6-only network reaches it through NAT64 (RFC 6052). The converter above works them out for any address.

Why is IPv4 still used if it ran out?

Because almost everything already speaks it, and NAT lets many devices share one public address. Running out made IPv4 addresses expensive - they are bought and sold - rather than unusable. IPv6 grows alongside it instead of replacing it overnight.

Should I turn IPv6 off?

Usually not. Modern systems are built expecting it, and turning it off can slow some connections or break features. If a particular network misbehaves over IPv6, that is a problem to fix in that network.
