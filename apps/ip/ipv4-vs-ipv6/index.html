IPv4 and IPv6, side by side

SizeIPv4: 32 bits. IPv6: 128 bits.
How manyIPv4: about 4.3 billion. IPv6: about 340 undecillion (3.4 × 1038).
Written asIPv4: four numbers, 203.0.113.7. IPv6: eight hexadecimal groups, with runs of zeros shortened to ::, 2001:db8::7.
DefinedIPv4: RFC 791, 1981. IPv6: RFC 2460 in 1998, now RFC 8200.
Private rangeIPv4: 10/8, 172.16/12, 192.168/16. IPv6: unique local fc00::/7.
SharingIPv4: usually one public address per home, shared through NAT. IPv6: usually a whole block per home, so each device can have its own.

Why are there two?

IPv4 was designed when 4.3 billion addresses looked like plenty. The regional registries began running out in 2011, and most have now handed out their last ordinary IPv4 blocks; the internet kept working by sharing addresses behind NAT. IPv6 was made so that every device could have an address of its own again. The two run side by side, and most networks speak both.

Which one does my connection use?

Each connection uses one or the other. When your network has both, most devices try IPv6 first and fall back to IPv4 if it is slower to answer. The answer at the top of this page is the one this visit actually used. See your IPv6 address.

Is IPv6 faster or safer?

Neither, by design. Speed depends on the route your traffic takes, not the address family, and both carry the same encrypted web traffic. IPv6 can be quicker where it avoids a provider’s NAT, and a home router should firewall both.
