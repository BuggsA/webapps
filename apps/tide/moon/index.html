    The moon and the tide

    The moon pulls the sea under it toward it and leaves the sea on the far side behind, so the ocean stands in two bulges that the Earth turns through each day. At new and full moon the sun pulls in line with the moon, and the tides run biggest: spring tides, which on most coasts come a day or two after. At the quarter moons the sun pulls across the moon, and the tides are smallest: neap tides.

    Questions

    Why does the moon change shape?
    Half of the moon is always lit by the sun. As it goes round the Earth we see different amounts of that lit half: none at new moon, all of it at full moon, half of it at the quarters. One round of phases takes 29.5 days.
    Why can I see the moon in the daytime?
    For most of the month the moon is up for part of the day. Only near new moon, when it is close to the sun, is it lost in the glare. A first quarter moon rises around midday; a last quarter moon is still up in the morning.
    Why is a full moon up all night?
    A full moon sits opposite the sun, so it rises around sunset and sets around sunrise. A new moon does the reverse and is up only with the sun.
    Is the moon the same shape everywhere?
    The same amount is lit everywhere on Earth at the same moment, but from the southern hemisphere the moon is seen the other way up, so a waxing moon is lit on the left instead of the right. This page draws it for your hemisphere.
